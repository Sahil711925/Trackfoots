How a log becomes evidence

Four steps, none of which touch your code.

STEP 01

Send

Your app posts an event to the ingest API — or a shipper tails an existing log file. A signed receipt comes back at write time.

STEP 02

Chain

Each entry is SHA-256 hashed together with the previous one. One edit breaks every hash after it.

STEP 03

Seal

Every hundred entries collapse into one RFC 6962 Merkle root, written once to a contract that refuses to overwrite it.

STEP 04

Export & verify

Any range exports as a bundle. The open verifier checks it offline and names the batch that failed.

Two questions, answered plainly

Including the ones with uncomfortable answers.

What does a passing verification actually prove?

That the record you hold is the record that existed when the root was published — nothing edited, deleted or reordered. It does not prove what was written was true, or who wrote it. A deleted line's content cannot be recovered from a hash; only its existence and position can be proven. The incident report says so on the page we hand over.

What if a customer asks you to delete their data from a log you anchored forever?

You don't delete the evidence, you make the payload unreadable: hash or salt the personal fields before the line enters the log, then destroy the salt. The chain, the root and the proof of what happened all survive; the person's identity does not. It's a design decision, not a button — and it's the one question in this space almost nobody has an answer for.

Can the attacker just delete everything before a root is published?

That window is real and we state it. Anything written through the ingest API is covered immediately, because the receipt leaves the box at write time. A line written straight to disk by a process we don't control is protected only from the next anchor — the staleness check raises an alert when anchoring stops, but it cannot un-delete what it never saw.
